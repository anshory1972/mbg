## 1. Bregman (2017): poverty as scarcity, and basic income as the answer

**Thesis.** The poor do not make bad decisions because of bad character. Scarcity uses up their mental bandwidth. The fix is to change the context they live in by giving them money, not to "fix" the people.

**Argument and evidence**

| Claim | Evidence cited |
|---|---|
| The poor borrow more, save less, smoke and drink more, and exercise less | Stated as stylised facts. The usual reading is Thatcher's "personality defect". |
| Poverty itself reduces cognitive capacity | Indian sugarcane farmers get about 60% of their annual income at harvest. The same farmers scored worse on IQ tests before the harvest than after. The effect is about **14 IQ points**, roughly like losing a night's sleep. (Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao, *Science* 2013) |
| The mechanism is "scarcity mentality" | Eldar Shafir (Princeton). Scarcity of money, time or food narrows attention to the immediate need, so long-run planning drops away. Bregman's analogy is a computer running 10 heavy programs at once. |
| Knowledge-based programmes don't work | A meta-analysis of **201 studies** found that financial-literacy training has almost no effect. Shafir: "like teaching someone to swim and then throwing them in a stormy sea". Nudges and SMS reminders are cheap, but they treat the symptoms. |
| An old idea with support from left and right | Basic income: Thomas More (*Utopia*, 1516), Martin Luther King, Milton Friedman. A monthly grant that covers basic needs, is unconditional, and carries no stigma. |
| It has worked before | **Dauphin, Manitoba (Mincome, 1974–78).** Every resident was guaranteed income above the poverty line. A new government shelved the results in about 2,000 boxes. About 25 years later, Evelyn Forget analysed them and found better school performance, **hospitalisations down 8.5%**, and less domestic violence and fewer mental-health complaints. People did not stop working. Only new mothers and students worked less, and the students stayed in school longer. |
| Poverty is expensive | Child poverty in the US costs an estimated **$500 billion a year** through health, school dropout and crime. |
| It is affordable | A **negative income tax** tops up anyone below the line. Net cost about **$175 billion**, roughly 1% of US GDP or a quarter of US military spending, to lift every poor American above the poverty line. |

**Wider point.** Basic income also means rethinking what work is for. Two polls he cites: only 13% of workers like their jobs (230,000 employees in 142 countries), and 37% of British workers think their job doesn't need to exist. Closing line: *"Poverty is not a lack of character. Poverty is a lack of cash."*

## 2. Stewart (2024): cash beats aid-agency projects

**Speaker's position.** Stewart worked in development for 30 years. He ran an NGO in Kabul and later, as a UK minister at DFID, oversaw a budget of about $20 billion a year. He was the "expert" his own talk criticises.

**Problem**

- The share of people in extreme poverty has fallen, mostly because of growth in China. In sub-Saharan Africa, though, the **number** of people in extreme poverty rose from **170 million (1980) to about 430 million today**.
- **East Africa menstrual-sanitation project.** The project cost $40,000 and came with a 100-page document: needs assessments, logframes, theories of change. What was built was two latrines and five plastic buckets, worth about $2,000. Staff said they hadn't simply given the head teacher the money because "he would steal it". Stewart's reply: *"We stole the money"* ($38,000 of the $40,000). The same money could have covered 20 times as many schools.
- The underlying mental model is "teach a man to fish", which produces patronising programmes that often achieve the opposite of their aims.

**Evidence for cash**

- **Rwanda–Burundi border.** An NGO, likely GiveDirectly, gave households a **one-off, unconditional $900**: not a loan and not microcredit. Afterwards there was more electricity, near-universal roofs and health insurance, and more children in school.
- **Hundreds of RCTs** consistently find lower child mortality and depression, and higher education, health, business activity, savings, income and investment. Spending on alcohol does **not** rise.
- **Multiplier of about 2.5.** Each $1 produces about $2.50 of benefit in the surrounding villages. This acts as a local fiscal stimulus. (Egger et al., *Econometrica* 2022, Kenya)

**Four reasons cash works**

1. **Capital, not knowledge.** Jeanne already knows how to run her shop. What she lacks is the money for stock, "the fishing hook".
2. **Needs differ.** One person wants a shop, another school fees, another health care, a cow, a tailoring business or a motorbike taxi.
3. **Efficiency.** People build with neighbours and local materials for a fraction of what a contractor or NGO would spend.
4. **Trust and dignity.** People own their choices, so they keep up and take pride in what they invest in.

**What makes cash feasible now**

- Feature phones cost $7–9, so money can go straight to a phone without intermediaries.
- AI-based targeting: **Togo** paid 100,000 people by phone within hours (the Novissi programme).
- Anticipatory cash: payments go out *before* a predicted flood, so families can move people and livestock.

**Limits he acknowledges.** Cash does not replace good government, roads, bridges, dams or better schools. It lets the poor *use* those public goods.

**Ask.** Individuals can give about $30 a month directly. Philanthropists could fund at country scale (Rwanda, Malawi) to persuade governments and agencies. He says global development spending is about **twice** what it would cost to end extreme poverty.

## 3. Comparison

| | Bregman | Stewart |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Rich countries (US, Canada, NL) | Low-income countries (Africa) |
| Instrument | Universal monthly basic income / negative income tax | Targeted lump-sum unconditional transfer |
| Main mechanism | Psychology: scarcity uses up cognitive bandwidth | Economics: capital constraint, heterogeneous needs, transaction costs |
| Key evidence | Mani et al. (2013), Dauphin Mincome, financial-literacy meta-analysis | GiveDirectly RCTs, local multiplier (Egger et al.), Togo Novissi |
| What they reject | Paternalism, nudges, financial literacy | Aid-agency overhead, capacity building, "teach to fish" |
| Common ground | Unconditional cash beats paternalistic in-kind or advisory help; trust and dignity matter | (same) |

## 4. Relevance to the MBG paper

MBG (*Makan Bergizi Gratis*) is an **in-kind** transfer delivered through public procurement. That is the kind of programme both talks argue against. This creates a natural counterfactual to compare it with:

- **Cash vs. in-kind with the same budget.** Our CGE results show that under the *protection* regime, food-price inflation takes away much of the transfer's value: real HH consumption is −0.59% while adjusted welfare is +0.79%. A cash transfer of equal fiscal cost spreads demand across all goods rather than concentrating it on food. It would probably create less food-CPI pressure and give households the choice Stewart argues for. *Possible new scenario: MBG budget delivered as BLT/BSU-style cash.*
- **Leakage and overhead.** Stewart's "$38k of $40k" example points to MBG's kitchen, SPPG and logistics costs. What share of each rupiah actually reaches a child as food, and how would that compare with cash at near-zero delivery cost through Indonesia's existing social-assistance rails (DTKS/DTSEN, PKH, BPNT)?
- **Multiplier and regional incidence.** Stewart's 2.5× local multiplier fits our finding that the transfer is most progressive in poor provinces: about +4.3pp of household consumption in NTT against +0.4pp in DKI. Regional cash transfers could have similar or larger local multipliers without the food-price spillover.
- **The case for in-kind (to be fair).** Arguments for MBG over cash: it targets *child* nutrition specifically, getting past who controls spending within the household; school meals raise attendance; and quality and fortification can be controlled. Stewart concedes that cash doesn't replace public goods. A fair comparison should weigh nutrition outcomes, not only consumption.
- **Scarcity channel.** Bregman's bandwidth argument suggests that income stability (regular transfers) may matter beyond the money itself. It is not captured in a CGE model, but it could go in the discussion.
